## Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis

**1 Disease Overview**
*   **Definition:** Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive, and ultimately fatal lung disease characterized by the formation of scar tissue (fibrosis) within the walls of the air sacs (alveoli) and the supporting structures (interstitium) of the lungs, leading to irreversible loss of lung function. The term "idiopathic" signifies that the exact cause remains unknown.
*   **Key Features:** Progressive exertional dyspnea (shortness of breath), persistent dry cough, bilateral basal inspiratory crackles ("Velcro-like" sound) on lung auscultation, and a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT).
*   **Disease Category/Class:** IPF is classified as a specific form of chronic fibrosing interstitial pneumonia (ILD), falling under the broader category of interstitial lung diseases.

**2 Synonyms & Abbreviations**
*   **Alternate Names:** Cryptogenic fibrosing alveolitis (CFA - historical term), Usual Interstitial Pneumonia (UIP - refers to the histopathological pattern, not entirely synonymous but often used interchangeably in clinical context for IPF).
*   **Preferred Acronym(s):** IPF.

**3 Subtypes / Variants**
*   **Subdivisions:** IPF itself is considered a distinct clinical entity without formally recognized molecular or pathological subtypes. However, clinical phenotypes exist based on disease behavior (e.g., rapid progressors vs. slow progressors) and comorbidities (e.g., IPF with frequent acute exacerbations, IPF with prominent emphysema - combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE)). These phenotypes are descriptive and not based on distinct etiologies or diagnostic criteria.

**4 Epidemiology**
*   **Prevalence / Incidence:** Incidence estimates range from 3 to 9 cases per 100,000 person-years in North America and Europe. Prevalence is estimated between 14 and 63 cases per 100,000 population. Estimates vary based on methodology and region (e.g., US data sources like registries and healthcare databases, European studies like IFIGENIA and EMPIRE). Data reflects studies published primarily within the last decade.
*   **Demographics:** IPF is predominantly a disease of older adults, with a median age at diagnosis typically between 60 and 70 years. It is more common in men than women, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1.5-2:1. A strong association with a history of cigarette smoking is well-established. While observed worldwide, some studies suggest potential geographic and ethnic variations, with higher reported rates in the UK and US compared to some other regions; the role of ethnicity requires further study.
*   **Rarity Status:** IPF is classified as a rare disease in the United States (affecting fewer than 200,000 individuals) and similarly in Europe (prevalence < 5 per 10,000).

**5 Etiology & Pathogenesis**
*   **Primary Cause(s):** The cause of IPF is unknown (idiopathic). It is believed to result from a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and aberrant wound healing responses in the lung epithelium of susceptible, aging individuals. No single infectious agent, autoimmune process, or toxin has been definitively proven as the sole cause.
*   **Inheritance Pattern:** Most cases are sporadic. However, familial clustering occurs in approximately 5-20% of cases, often with autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance. Mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance (e.g., *TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*, *RTEL1*, *DKC1*, *TINF2*) and surfactant protein processing (e.g., *SFTPC*, *SFTPA2*) are the most strongly associated with familial and some sporadic IPF.
*   **Pathophysiologic Mechanism:** The prevailing hypothesis is that recurrent, microscopic injury to the alveolar epithelium (potentially driven by factors like cigarette smoke, environmental dusts, microaspiration, or intrinsic aging/genetic defects) in a susceptible host leads to dysfunctional repair. This involves aberrant activation and proliferation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (collagen), and the formation of fibroblastic foci. Chronic inflammation may be present but is not considered the primary driver; epithelial cell senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic reprogramming are increasingly recognized contributors.
*   **Key Risk Factors:** Advanced age (>60 years), male sex, cigarette smoking (current or former), certain occupational/environmental exposures (e.g., metal dust, wood dust, farming/livestock, stone cutting/polishing), genetic predisposition (family history or identified mutations), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) are established risk factors.

**6 Clinical Presentation**
*   **Core Signs & Symptoms:**
    *   **Most Common/Early:** Insidious onset of exertional dyspnea (shortness of breath during activity) is the cardinal symptom, often initially dismissed as aging or deconditioning. A persistent, typically non-productive (dry) cough is also very common early on. Fatigue and general malaise are frequent complaints.
    *   **Progressive Symptoms:** Dyspnea progressively worsens over time, occurring with less and less exertion and eventually at rest. The dry cough becomes more persistent and bothersome. Weight loss and loss of appetite may develop.
    *   **Late Signs & Symptoms:** Cyanosis (bluish discoloration of lips/skin due to low oxygen), digital clubbing (enlargement of fingertips and nails), symptoms of right heart failure (cor pulmonale) such as peripheral edema (swelling in legs/ankles), prominent jugular venous distension, and eventually respiratory failure requiring supplemental oxygen continuously.
*   **Progression Pattern:** IPF follows a chronic progressive course in most patients, though the rate of progression is highly variable. Some patients experience periods of relative stability punctuated by stepwise declines, while others decline steadily. A significant proportion of patients (estimated 5-15% per year) suffer acute exacerbations (AE-IPF), characterized by sudden, clinically significant respiratory deterioration (worsening dyspnea over days to weeks) with new diffuse lung opacities on imaging, often leading to respiratory failure and death.
*   **Variability between patients:** The rate of functional decline varies considerably, with some patients progressing rapidly over months ("rapid progressors") and others declining more slowly over several years ("slow progressors"). The frequency and severity of acute exacerbations also vary. Symptom severity does not always correlate perfectly with the extent of fibrosis on imaging.
*   **Major Complications:** Acute exacerbations of IPF (AE-IPF), respiratory failure (hypoxemic and/or hypercapnic), pulmonary hypertension (PH), cor pulmonale (right heart failure secondary to lung disease), lung cancer (significantly increased risk compared to general population), and complications related to chronic hypoxemia and immobility (e.g., muscle wasting, venous thromboembolism).

**7 Diagnostic Evaluation**
*   **Clinical Criteria:** Key findings raising suspicion include unexplained exertional dyspnea and/or chronic dry cough in an individual typically over 60 years old with risk factors (smoking, male sex). Physical examination typically reveals bibasilar, end-inspiratory "Velcro-like" crackles on lung auscultation. Digital clubbing may be present.
*   **Laboratory Tests:** There is no single diagnostic blood biomarker. Testing often includes serology to exclude connective tissue disease-associated ILD (e.g., ANA, RF, anti-CCP, myositis panel) and other potential causes. Elevated biomarkers like KL-6 or SP-D may support the diagnosis or indicate activity but are not specific or widely standardized for routine diagnosis.
*   **Imaging / Instrumental Tests:**
    *   **High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT):** The cornerstone of diagnosis. A definite UIP pattern on HRCT (subpleural, basal predominant reticulation, honeycombing with or without traction bronchiectasis, absence of features inconsistent with UIP) in the appropriate clinical context is sufficient for diagnosis without biopsy.
    *   **Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs):** Essential for assessment and monitoring. Typically show a restrictive ventilatory defect (reduced FVC, TLC) and impaired gas exchange (reduced DLCO).
    *   **6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT):** Assesses functional capacity and oxygen requirements; desaturation during the test is common and prognostically significant.
    *   **Surgical Lung Biopsy (SLB):** Required if the HRCT pattern is indeterminate or probable UIP, or if features are inconsistent with UIP, and clinical suspicion remains high. Histopathology must show a UIP pattern (patchy fibrosis, fibroblastic foci, honeycombing, architectural distortion, temporal heterogeneity).
*   **Genetic Testing:** Recommended for patients with a family history of ILD or features suggestive of telomere biology disorders (e.g., premature graying, unexplained cytopenias). Panels testing genes like *TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*, *RTEL1*, *SFTPC*, *SFTPA2*, *DKC1*, *TINF2* are used. Results can inform prognosis and family counseling but are not required for diagnosis in sporadic cases.
*   **Formal Guidelines:** Diagnosis should follow the most current international consensus guidelines, primarily the joint American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society (ERS)/Japanese Respiratory Society (JRS)/Latin American Thoracic Association (ALAT) Clinical Practice Guideline (e.g., Raghu et al. 2018, Am J Respir Crit Care Med). These provide detailed algorithms integrating clinical, radiological (HRCT), and histopathological findings.

**8 Management & Standard Therapy**
*   **First-Line Treatments:** Antifibrotic medications are the cornerstone of pharmacotherapy to slow disease progression:
    *   **Pirfenidone:** 801 mg three times daily (titrated up over 2 weeks). Common side effects include nausea, rash, photosensitivity, fatigue, and anorexia.
    *   **Nintedanib:** 150 mg twice daily. Common side effects include diarrhea, nausea, abdominal pain, vomiting, and elevated liver enzymes (requires monitoring).
*   **Second-Line / Adjunctive:** If one antifibrotic is poorly tolerated, switching to the other may be considered. There is no established second-line antifibrotic. Immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., prednisone, azathioprine, mycophenolate) is generally contraindicated in IPF due to lack of efficacy and potential harm (as shown in PANTHER-IPF trial), except in specific contexts like acute exacerbations (controversial) or coexisting connective tissue disease. Lung transplantation remains the only curative intervention for eligible patients.
*   **Supportive Care:**
    *   **Oxygen Therapy:** Prescribed for hypoxemia at rest, during exertion, or during sleep to improve symptoms and potentially survival.
    *   **Pulmonary Rehabilitation:** Structured exercise program, education, and nutritional/psychological support to improve exercise capacity, reduce symptoms, and enhance quality of life.
    *   **Symptom Management:** Antitussives for refractory cough (limited efficacy), opioids for severe dyspnea (palliative use), management of GERD (PPIs/H2 blockers).
    *   **Vaccinations:** Annual influenza vaccine and pneumococcal vaccines (PCV13/PPSV23) are crucial to prevent respiratory infections.
    *   **Monitoring:** Regular clinical assessment, serial PFTs (e.g., FVC, DLCO every 3-6 months), HRCT as clinically indicated, and monitoring for complications (PH, lung cancer).
*   **Preventive Measures:** Smoking cessation is paramount. Avoidance of known occupational/environmental lung irritants when possible. Aggressive management of GERD is often recommended, though evidence for preventing progression is limited. Vaccinations as above.

**9 Investigational / Emerging Therapies**
*   **Therapies in Trials:** Numerous novel agents targeting various pathways implicated in IPF pathogenesis are under investigation in clinical trials. These include:
    *   **Anti-fibrotics:** Next-generation agents with potentially improved efficacy or tolerability profiles (e.g., PLN-74809 - αvβ6/αvβ1 integrin inhibitor).
    *   **Anti-inflammatory/Immunomodulatory:** Targeting specific pro-fibrotic cytokines or immune cells (e.g., Pamrevlumab - anti-CTGF monoclonal antibody, BG00011 - anti-αvβ6 integrin antibody).
    *   **Anti-fibrotic Biologics:** Pentraxin-2 (PRM-151), a recombinant human protein modulating monocyte differentiation.
    *   **Lysophosphatidic Acid (LPA) Receptor Antagonists:** BMS-986020, BMS-986278.
    *   **Autotaxin Inhibitors:** GLPG1690 (Ziritaxestat - Phase 3 failed, others in earlier stages).
    *   **Senolytics:** Drugs targeting senescent cells (e.g., Dasatinib + Quercetin).
    *   **Gene Therapy/Cell-Based Therapies:** Early preclinical/phase 1 exploration.
*   **Trial Resources:** Current clinical trials can be searched on registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov), EU Clinical Trials Register (https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu), and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP - https://trialsearch.who.int/).

**10 Prognosis**
*   **Natural History:** IPF has a poor prognosis. Historically, median survival from time of diagnosis was estimated at 2-5 years without antifibrotic treatment. The disease course is unpredictable but generally progressive, leading to respiratory failure. Acute exacerbations significantly worsen prognosis.
*   **Impact of Therapy:** Antifibrotic therapy (pirfenidone and nintedanib) has been shown to significantly slow the rate of lung function decline (measured by FVC) and reduce the risk of acute exacerbations. While not curative, these treatments extend median survival compared to placebo (estimates suggest adding 2-3 years on average). Lung transplantation offers the best chance for long-term survival in eligible patients, with median post-transplant survival exceeding 5-7 years.
*   **Prognostic Factors:**
    *   **Worse Prognosis:** Older age, male sex, lower baseline FVC % predicted, lower baseline DLCO % predicted, significant desaturation during 6MWT, presence of pulmonary hypertension, frequent hospitalizations, history of acute exacerbation, rapid decline in FVC (>10% in 6-12 months), definite UIP pattern on HRCT or biopsy, presence of honeycombing, specific genetic mutations (e.g., telomere-related).
    *   **Better Prognosis:** Younger age, female sex, preserved lung function at diagnosis, slower rate of FVC decline, absence of pulmonary hypertension or significant comorbidities. Response to antifibrotic therapy stabilizes decline and improves prognosis.
