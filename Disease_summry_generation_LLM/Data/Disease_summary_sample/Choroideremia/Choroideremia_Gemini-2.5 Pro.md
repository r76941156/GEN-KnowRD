Choroideremia is a progressive, X-linked chorioretinal dystrophy characterized by the degeneration of the choroid, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and retina. This ultimately leads to severe vision loss and blindness. 

### 1. Disease Overview 

Choroideremia is a rare, inherited disorder that primarily affects males and causes a gradual loss of vision. The disease process involves the progressive atrophy of several crucial layers at the back of the eye, including the photoreceptors, the RPE, and the choroid, which is a network of blood vessels that nourishes the retina.

**Key Features:** 
*   Progressive night blindness beginning in childhood. 
*   Loss of peripheral vision leading to tunnel vision. 
*   Eventual loss of central vision and color perception. 

**Disease Category/Class:** 
Choroideremia is a genetic, inherited retinal degeneration. 

### 2. Synonyms & Abbreviations 

**Alternate Names:** 
*   Choroidal sclerosis 
*   Progressive tapetochoroidal dystrophy 
*   Progressive choroidal atrophy 
*   Tapetochoroidal dystrophy 

**Preferred Acronym(s):** 
*   CHM 

### 3. Subtypes / Variants 

No formal subtypes of Choroideremia have been reported. 

### 4. Epidemiology 

**Prevalence / Incidence:** 
The prevalence of choroideremia is estimated to be between 1 in 50,000 and 1 in 100,000 individuals. It is believed that the condition may be underdiagnosed due to its similarity to other eye disorders. The highest reported prevalence is in Northern Finland. 

**Demographics:** 
The initial symptom, night blindness, typically manifests in the first decade of life. Due to its X-linked inheritance, choroideremia predominantly affects males. 

**Rarity Status:** 
Choroideremia is classified as a rare disease. 

### 5. Etiology & Pathogenesis 

**Primary Cause(s):** 
Choroideremia is caused by mutations in the *CHM* gene. These are typically loss-of-function mutations that result in an absent or non-functional protein. 

**Inheritance Pattern:** 
The condition is inherited in an X-linked recessive manner. This means that males, who have only one X chromosome, will be affected if they inherit the mutated gene. Affected males will pass the mutated gene to all of their daughters, who will be carriers, but not to their sons. Female carriers have a 50% chance of passing the mutated gene to their children. 

**Pathophysiologic Mechanism:** 
The *CHM* gene provides instructions for making Rab escort protein 1 (REP-1). REP-1 plays a critical role in intracellular trafficking by escorting Rab proteins to their target membranes. A deficiency in functional REP-1 disrupts these essential cellular processes, leading to premature cell death. Although REP-1 is present throughout the body, a similar protein called REP-2 can compensate for its absence in non-ocular tissues. In the retina, however, REP-2 is unable to fully compensate for the loss of REP-1, leading to the progressive degeneration of the RPE, photoreceptors, and choroid. 

**Key Risk Factors:** 
The primary risk factor for choroideremia is having a family history of the condition. 

### 6. Clinical Presentation 

**Core Signs & Symptoms:** 
The clinical presentation of choroideremia progresses through distinct stages: 
*   **Early Childhood:** The first symptom is typically nyctalopia, or difficulty seeing in low light, which often appears before the age of 10. 
*   **Adolescence and Early Adulthood:** Progressive loss of peripheral vision begins, often manifesting as a ring scotoma (a ring-shaped area of vision loss). This gradually constricts the visual field, leading to tunnel vision. 
*   **Mid-Adulthood:** While central visual acuity is often preserved until the 40s or 50s, it eventually deteriorates. Loss of color vision also occurs as the macula degenerates. 
*   **Late Adulthood:** The disease progresses to legal blindness, and in many cases, total blindness. 

**Progression Pattern:** 
Choroideremia is a chronic progressive disease. The rate of progression can vary significantly among affected individuals, even within the same family. 

**Variability between patients:** 
There can be striking phenotypic variation within a single family, with some males being significantly less affected than others. Female carriers are generally asymptomatic, but may show signs of retinal pigment epithelial depigmentation or atrophy on careful fundus examination. In rare instances, female carriers may experience symptoms if the healthy copy of the *CHM* gene is inactivated through a process called skewed X-chromosome inactivation. 

**Major Complications:** 
The major complication of choroideremia is progressive vision loss leading to blindness. Cataracts and cystoid macular edema have also been reported. 

### 7. Diagnostic Evaluation 

**Clinical Criteria:** 
The diagnosis of choroideremia is suspected based on the characteristic clinical findings, including night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and a family history consistent with X-linked inheritance. Fundus examination reveals distinctive changes, starting with pigmentary stippling and progressing to widespread atrophy of the choroid and RPE, exposing the underlying sclera. 

**Laboratory Tests:** 
While not a primary diagnostic tool, some studies have noted fatty acid abnormalities in the leukocytes of patients with choroideremia. 

**Imaging / Instrumental Tests:** 
Several imaging and electrophysiological tests are used to confirm the diagnosis and monitor disease progression: 
*   **Fundus Autofluorescence (FAF):** This imaging technique can identify stress or damage to the RPE and is used to track the progression of atrophy. 
*   **Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT):** OCT provides detailed cross-sectional images of the retina, revealing thinning of the subfoveal retina and choroid as the disease progresses. It can also detect complications like cystoid macular edema. 
*   **Electroretinography (ERG):** An ERG measures the electrical response of the retinal cells to light. In choroideremia, the ERG initially shows a pattern of rod-cone degeneration, eventually becoming non-recordable. 
*   **Fluorescein Angiography:** This test uses a dye to visualize the blood vessels in the eye and can confirm the loss of the choriocapillaris. 
*   **Visual Field Testing:** This test maps the field of vision and documents the progressive constriction characteristic of the disease. 
*   **Microperimetry:** This technique assesses retinal sensitivity and can detect functional decline before changes in visual acuity are apparent. 

**Genetic Testing:** 
The diagnosis is confirmed by molecular genetic testing that identifies a pathogenic variant in the *CHM* gene. Genetic testing is crucial for differentiating choroideremia from other similar retinal dystrophies, such as retinitis pigmentosa and gyrate atrophy. 

**Formal Guidelines:** 
Diagnosis is typically based on the combination of clinical findings, imaging results, and confirmatory genetic testing. 

### 8. Management & Standard Therapy 

**First-Line Treatments:** 
There is currently no cure or approved treatment to stop the progression of choroideremia. Management focuses on supportive care and addressing complications. 

**Second-Line / Adjunctive:** 
Frank macular edema, though not typical, can cause a sudden decline in central vision and may be treated with topical dorzolamide. 

**Supportive Care:** 
*   **Low Vision Aids:** Magnifiers, high-contrast reading materials, and electronic devices can help patients maintain functional vision. 
*   **UV Protection:** Wearing UV-blocking sunglasses is recommended to protect the remaining retinal cells. 
*   **Dietary Supplements:** Some studies have investigated the use of lutein supplements, which may increase macular pigment density, but a direct benefit on slowing disease progression has not been definitively established. A diet rich in fresh fruits, leafy green vegetables, and omega-3 fatty acids is also suggested. 
*   **Counseling:** Genetic counseling is important for understanding the inheritance pattern and reproductive options. Psychological counseling can help patients and families cope with the emotional and social challenges of progressive vision loss. 

**Preventive Measures:** 
Avoiding smoking is recommended, as it is a major risk factor for macular degeneration. 

### 9. Investigational / Emerging Therapies 

**Therapies in Trials:** 
Gene therapy is the most promising area of research for treating choroideremia. The goal is to deliver a functional copy of the *CHM* gene to the retinal cells using a viral vector, typically an adeno-associated virus (AAV). 
*   **AAV2-REP1 (timrepigene emparvovec):** Several clinical trials have investigated the safety and efficacy of subretinal injection of AAV2 vectors carrying the *CHM* gene. Early phase trials showed promise, with some patients experiencing improvements in visual acuity. However, a large-scale Phase 3 trial did not meet its primary endpoint, though some patients did show vision improvement. 
*   **Retinal Prosthesis:** For patients in advanced stages of the disease, retinal prosthesis systems like the Argus II have been explored to provide a form of artificial vision. 
*   **Stem Cell Therapy:** Research is ongoing into the potential of using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to generate new retinal cells for transplantation. 

**Trial Resources:** 
Information on clinical trials for choroideremia can be found on registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov. 

### 10. Prognosis 

**Natural History:** 
Without treatment, choroideremia leads to a progressive decline in vision. Night blindness in childhood is followed by concentric visual field loss, culminating in blindness, typically in late adulthood. Studies analyzing the natural history have shown a logarithmic decline in macular sensitivity, with a half-life of approximately 14-15 years. The loss of the residual RPE area follows an exponential decay pattern over about 60 years. 

**Impact of Therapy:** 
Currently, there is no therapy that has been proven to alter the long-term prognosis of choroideremia. However, the goal of emerging treatments like gene therapy is to slow or halt the degenerative process, thereby preserving vision for a longer period. 

**Prognostic Factors:** 
The rate of disease progression can vary, but studies have shown a high degree of symmetry in the decline of visual function between a patient's two eyes, making the untreated eye a good control in clinical trials. The proximity of the advancing edge of retinal atrophy to the fovea is a key factor in predicting the decline of central vision.
