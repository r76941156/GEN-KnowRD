## Choroideremia

**1 Disease Overview**
*   **Definition:** Choroideremia is a rare, inherited retinal dystrophy characterized by progressive degeneration of the choroid, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and photoreceptors (rods and cones), leading to significant vision loss. It primarily affects the light-sensing tissue at the back of the eye.
*   **Key Features:** Progressive nyctalopia (night blindness), concentric peripheral visual field loss, and characteristic fundus appearance with chorioretinal atrophy.
*   **Disease Category/Class:** X-linked recessive inherited retinal dystrophy (chorioretinal degeneration).

**2 Synonyms & Abbreviations**
*   **Alternate Names:** Tapetochoroidal dystrophy (historical), CHM (often used synonymously for the disease based on the gene).
*   **Preferred Acronym(s):** CHM.

**3 Subtypes / Variants**
*   **Subdivisions:** No formal disease subtypes are recognized. Phenotypic variability exists in terms of age of onset and progression rate. Female carriers are usually asymptomatic or minimally affected but can exhibit fundus changes (focal areas of RPE mottling or atrophy) and rarely experience significant vision loss.

**4 Epidemiology**
*   **Prevalence / Incidence:** Estimated prevalence is approximately 1 in 50,000 to 1 in 100,000 individuals. Incidence is estimated around 1 in 150,000 live births. (Sources: Orphanet, epidemiological studies; figures represent global estimates).
*   **Demographics:** Onset typically in childhood or adolescence (first symptoms often noticed in the first or second decade). Almost exclusively affects males due to X-linked inheritance. Female carriers are generally unaffected clinically. No specific ethnic or geographic clustering is established.
*   **Rarity Status:** Classified as a rare disease in the US (affecting <200,000 individuals), the EU (<1 in 2,000), and globally.

**5 Etiology & Pathogenesis**
*   **Primary Cause(s):** Mutations in the *CHM* gene (located on Xq21.2) encoding Rab escort protein 1 (REP-1).
*   **Inheritance Pattern:** X-linked recessive.
*   **Pathophysiologic Mechanism:** Loss of functional REP-1 disrupts the prenylation (lipid modification) of Rab GTPases, essential regulators of intracellular vesicular trafficking. This leads to impaired protein trafficking, particularly in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptors, resulting in accumulation of waste products, failure of phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments, and ultimately progressive apoptosis (cell death) of the choroid, RPE, and photoreceptors.
*   **Key Risk Factors:** Male sex and positive family history are the primary risk factors. No established environmental or lifestyle contributors have been identified.

**6 Clinical Presentation**
*   **Core Signs & Symptoms:**
    *   **Early Childhood/Adolescence:** The earliest and most common symptom is progressive nyctalopia (night blindness), often noticeable in the first or second decade. Patients may also report mild peripheral visual field constriction initially.
    *   **Adolescence/Young Adulthood:** Progressive concentric peripheral visual field loss (tunnel vision) becomes more pronounced. Difficulties with dark adaptation and mobility in low light are significant. Central vision is typically preserved early on.
    *   **Adulthood:** Peripheral visual field loss continues to advance, leading to severe constriction. Central visual acuity begins to decline as macular involvement progresses. Patients experience increasing photophobia (light sensitivity). Color vision may become impaired, particularly in the blue-yellow axis, as cone function deteriorates.
    *   **Late Stages:** Profound peripheral vision loss leaves only a small central island of vision. Significant central visual acuity loss occurs, often progressing to legal blindness (visual acuity worse than 20/200 and/or visual field less than 20 degrees). Complete loss of vision can occur, typically in the 5th to 7th decade. Fundus examination reveals widespread, well-demarcated areas of chorioretinal atrophy exposing the underlying sclera, initially in the mid-periphery and later involving the macula and extreme periphery, with residual islands of preserved RPE.
*   **Progression Pattern:** Chronic, progressive, and irreversible degeneration. The rate of progression is variable but generally relentless, leading to significant visual impairment over decades.
*   **Variability between patients:** Considerable inter- and intra-familial variability exists in the age of symptom onset and the rate of progression. Some individuals experience relatively stable central vision until later adulthood, while others progress more rapidly.
*   **Major Complications:** Profound visual impairment and legal blindness are the primary complications, significantly impacting independence, mobility, employment, and quality of life. Complete blindness can occur.

**7 Diagnostic Evaluation**
*   **Clinical Criteria:** Key findings include early nyctalopia, progressive peripheral visual field loss, and the characteristic fundus appearance (bilateral, symmetrical, progressive chorioretinal atrophy starting in the mid-periphery, sparing the macula initially, and exposing large choroidal vessels and sclera).
*   **Laboratory Tests:** No specific serum biomarkers exist. Electroretinography (ERG) is crucial, showing severely reduced or non-detectable rod responses early in the disease course, followed by progressive cone system dysfunction (reduced or extinguished scotopic and photopic responses).
*   **Imaging / Instrumental Tests:**
    *   **Fundus Autofluorescence (FAF):** Shows characteristic patterns: early hyperautofluorescence at the leading edge of atrophy, hypoautofluorescence in areas of atrophy, and preserved autofluorescence in unaffected areas. Helps track progression.
    *   **Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT):** Reveals thinning of the outer retina, RPE, and choroid; loss of photoreceptor layers (ellipsoid zone, interdigitation zone); and hypertransmission of signal into the choroid/sclera in areas of atrophy.
    *   **Visual Field Testing (Perimetry):** Goldmann or static perimetry demonstrates progressive concentric constriction of the peripheral visual fields.
    *   **Fluorescein Angiography (FA) / Indocyanine Green Angiography (ICGA):** May show window defects in areas of RPE loss and choroidal atrophy but are less routinely used than FAF and OCT. ICGA can highlight choroidal vessel loss.
*   **Genetic Testing:** Molecular genetic testing of the *CHM* gene is the gold standard for confirmation. Sequence analysis detects point mutations and small indels. Deletion/duplication analysis identifies larger deletions. Carrier testing and prenatal diagnosis are available for families with a known mutation.
*   **Formal Guidelines:** While no single universally mandated guideline exists, diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on the constellation of clinical presentation, family history, electrophysiology (ERG), retinal imaging (FAF, OCT), and molecular genetic testing, as outlined in reviews and expert consensus (e.g., publications by the European Retinal Disease Consortium, RetNet).

**8 Management & Standard Therapy**
*   **First-Line Treatments:** There are currently no approved disease-modifying therapies to halt or reverse degeneration. Management is supportive and focuses on maximizing remaining vision and function.
*   **Second-Line / Adjunctive:** Not applicable for the underlying degeneration. Management of complications like cataract may be considered if visually significant.
*   **Supportive Care:**
    *   **Low Vision Rehabilitation:** Comprehensive assessment and provision of low vision aids (magnifiers, telescopes, electronic devices), adaptive skills training, and orientation & mobility training.
    *   **Genetic Counseling:** Essential for affected individuals and their families to understand inheritance patterns, recurrence risks, and options for carrier testing and prenatal diagnosis.
    *   **Psychosocial Support:** Counseling and support groups to address emotional well-being and adjustment to vision loss.
    *   **Regular Ophthalmologic Monitoring:** Annual or more frequent examinations including visual acuity, visual fields, fundus imaging (FAF, OCT), and ERG to monitor progression and guide supportive care.
*   **Preventive Measures:** No proven preventive measures exist. UV-protective sunglasses may help manage photophobia. Genetic counseling is the primary strategy for prevention in families.

**9 Investigational / Emerging Therapies**
*   **Therapies in Trials:** Gene therapy is the most advanced investigational approach. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are used to deliver a functional copy of the *CHM* gene to retinal cells (subretinal injection). Several Phase 1/2 and Phase 3 clinical trials have been conducted or are ongoing (e.g., trials sponsored by Spark Therapeutics/Novartis, 4D Molecular Therapeutics, STZ eyetrial). Other approaches under preclinical or early clinical investigation include RNA-based therapies (e.g., antisense oligonucleotides for specific mutations), small molecules targeting the prenylation pathway or downstream effects, and stem cell therapies aiming to replace lost RPE/photoreceptors.
*   **Trial Resources:**
    *   ClinicalTrials.gov (search terms: Choroideremia, CHM, Gene Therapy)
    *   EU Clinical Trials Register
    *   International Standard Randomised Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry
    *   Choroideremia Research Foundation (funding research and providing trial information)

**10 Prognosis**
*   **Natural History:** Progressive and irreversible vision loss. Nyctalopia begins in childhood/adolescence, significant peripheral field loss develops in adolescence/adulthood, central vision deteriorates in adulthood, often leading to legal blindness by the 5th to 7th decade. Complete blindness can occur.
*   **Impact of Therapy:** Current management (supportive care) does not alter the underlying disease progression or long-term visual prognosis. It aims to optimize function and quality of life. Gene therapy trials show promise in potentially slowing or stabilizing vision loss in some treated individuals, but long-term efficacy and safety data are still being gathered; it is not yet a standard cure.
*   **Prognostic Factors:** Rate of progression is variable. Factors potentially associated with faster progression include specific mutation types (e.g., null mutations), earlier age of symptom onset, and more extensive chorioretinal atrophy at presentation. Preservation of central retinal structure (on OCT) and function (central visual acuity) are positive prognostic indicators for longer retention of useful central vision. Female carriers generally have an excellent visual prognosis.
