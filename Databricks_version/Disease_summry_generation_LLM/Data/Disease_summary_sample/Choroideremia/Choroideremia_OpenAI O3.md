1 Disease Overview  
Choroideremia is a hereditary retinal dystrophy in which the vascular layer of the eye (choroid), the retinal pigment epithelium and the photoreceptors undergo progressive, centripetal degeneration that eventually causes blindness, most often in adult men. The hallmark clinical characteristics are early‐onset night-blindness, progressive concentric loss of peripheral visual field and late loss of central acuity. The disorder belongs to the class of X-linked genetic retinal degenerations. 2 Synonyms & Abbreviations  
Historically the condition has been called tapetochoroidal dystrophy, choroidal sclerosis, progressive choroidal atrophy and Rab-escort-protein-1 deficiency; “CHM” is the official gene symbol and is also used as a disease acronym. The currently preferred abbreviation in the ophthalmic and genetics literature is CHM for both the gene and the disease. ([orpha.net](https://www.orpha.net/en/disease/detail/180?utm_source=chatgpt.com))  

3 Subtypes / Variants  
No formal clinical subtypes have been recognized. Phenotypic variability does occur and correlates partly with the class of CHM pathogenic variant (null versus hypomorphic alleles) but these differences have not been codified into distinct sub-entities. 4 Epidemiology  
Large registry and administrative-dataset analyses place prevalence between one in 50 000 and one in 100 000 inhabitants in Europe and North America. A 2024 EBSCO Research Starter review and Orphanet consensus statement both cite this range, derived from population screening studies published in several Western countries. ([orpha.net](https://www.orpha.net/en/disease/detail/180?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [ebsco.com](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/consumer-health/choroideremia?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Almost all symptomatic patients are male; female carriers represent roughly two thirds of all genetically identified individuals but most remain minimally affected. Symptom onset, typically nyctalopia, occurs in the first or second decade, with legal blindness (visual field < 20° or acuity < 20⁄200) usually reached in the fifth decade. There is no known ethnic predilection, although ascertainment is highest in populations with access to molecular diagnostics. Because the U.S. patient pool is well under 200 000, choroideremia is officially classified as a rare disease. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32471821/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [fightingblindness.ca](https://www.fightingblindness.ca/eyehealth/eye-diseases/choroideremia/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))  

5 Etiology & Pathogenesis  
Choroideremia results from loss-of-function pathogenic variants in the CHM gene on Xq21.2 that encodes Rab escort protein-1 (REP-1). Inheritance is X-linked recessive. REP-1 is required for geranylgeranyl prenylation of Rab GTPases; without prenylation, Rabs cannot anchor to intracellular membranes, disrupting vesicular trafficking in the retinal pigment epithelium and photoreceptors. Progressive RPE death precedes secondary photoreceptor and choroidal atrophy, explaining the rod-cone pattern of degeneration. Oxidative stress, ultraviolet light exposure and cigarette smoking are considered modifiable cofactors that may accelerate choroidal thinning, although they are not primary causes. ([ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK604018/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8287038/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [journalretinavitreous.biomedcentral.com](https://journalretinavitreous.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40942-025-00646-9?utm_source=chatgpt.com))  

6 Clinical Presentation  
The earliest and most common symptom is impaired scotopic vision in dim light, often before age ten. Peripheral field constriction follows during adolescence, producing so-called tunnel vision in the third and fourth decades. Central visual acuity and color discrimination remain relatively preserved until degeneration reaches the fovea, usually after age forty, when a more rapid decline begins. Fundus examination shows patchy RPE depigmentation, pigment clumping distinct from bone-spicule retinitis pigmentosa, and well-demarcated areas of choroidal atrophy that advance centripetally. Ancillary findings include posterior subcapsular cataract in about one third of patients, cystoid macular edema detectable on OCT, and occasional choroidal neovascularization. Female heterozygotes vary from asymptomatic to mild nyctalopia with mottled peripheral RPE changes, depending on X-inactivation. Major long-term complications are severe concentric field loss, legal blindness typically by the sixth decade and psychosocial disability related to progressive vision loss. 7 Diagnostic Evaluation  
Diagnosis is suspected on clinical grounds when a young male presents with night-blindness, an X-linked pedigree and characteristic fundus autofluorescence showing peripheral hypo-autofluorescent atrophy with central island sparing. Full-field electroretinography demonstrates a rod-predominant reduction. Spectral-domain OCT reveals loss of the ellipsoid zone and outer retinal tubulations adjacent to intact foveal tissue; ultra-widefield fundus autofluorescence is emerging as a quantitative biomarker of residual RPE. Genetic confirmation is obtained by sequencing of CHM with deletion/duplication analysis; a single-gene test or inclusion of CHM on inherited retinal dystrophy panels is acceptable, and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification is advised when no sequence variant is found. No formal international diagnostic guideline exists, but GeneReviews provides consensus recommendations for baseline ophthalmic, imaging and genetic work-up. ([ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK1337/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1337/?utm_source=chatgpt.com), [orpha.net](https://www.orpha.net/en/disease/detail/180?utm_source=chatgpt.com))  

8 Management & Standard Therapy  
There is no approved disease-modifying therapy. Current standard care is supportive: regular low-vision rehabilitation, orientation and mobility training, prescription of high-contrast visual aids, and teaching of UV-blocking and glare-reducing strategies. Annual ophthalmic follow-up with visual field testing, OCT and autofluorescence imaging is advised to track progression and detect treatable complications. Topical or oral carbonic-anhydrase inhibitors such as dorzolamide 2 % three times daily or acetazolamide 125–250 mg two to three times daily can reduce cystoid macular edema and may provide modest acuity benefit. Cataract extraction is performed when lens opacity limits residual central vision. Lifestyle counselling includes avoidance of tobacco smoke and excessive sunlight; smokers are counselled on cessation because smoking is associated with choroidal thinning. 9 Investigational / Emerging Therapies  
Adeno-associated-virus mediated gene augmentation with AAV2-REP1 (also known as BIIB111 or timrepigene emparvovec) has reached international phase III trials after phase I/II studies showed sustained visual-acuity gains of 3–10 ETDRS letters in a subset of treated eyes over five years, with intraocular inflammation as the chief risk. An intravitreal capsid-engineered vector (4D-110) is in phase I/II testing. Antisense oligonucleotide therapy targeting deep-intronic splice variants has restored full-length CHM transcript in patient fibroblasts and is in pre-clinical development. Small-molecule translational-read-through compounds to bypass premature stop codons have shown efficacy in zebrafish chm-null models. Retinal-prosthesis systems such as IRIS II or supra-choroidal bionic eye devices are being evaluated for end-stage disease without functional photoreceptors. Cell-replacement approaches remain experimental. Trial information is available on ClinicalTrials.gov under identifiers NCT03496012, NCT05144397 and related European counterparts. 10 Prognosis  
Without intervention, visual acuity follows a biphasic natural course: a slow decline of approximately 0.3 ETDRS letters per year until a transition age near 39 years, followed by a faster loss of about 1.2 letters per year. Most affected men retain serviceable central vision into their forties but become legally blind by 60–70 years, although peripheral field is often profoundly constricted earlier. Emerging gene therapy may stabilize or modestly improve acuity, particularly when delivered before the rapid decline phase, and could extend functional vision by a decade or more if long-term safety is confirmed. Better prognosis is associated with younger age at treatment, greater baseline area of preserved autofluorescence and absence of significant macular edema; smoking and late presentation predict faster deterioration.
